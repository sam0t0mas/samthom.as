The journey

From curiosity to consequence.

The roles changed, but the underlying pattern remained: understand the system, take responsibility, and make it work better.

1987—1999

The Tinkerer

Before technology was a career, it was simply what I did. The IBM XT opened the door, then came the 386 and 486 clones, web design and two Linux servers called Wallace and Gromit. I learned by pulling things apart, rebuilding them and trying again. At the same time I was working, leading the student council and discovering that responsibility matters as much as technical ability.

First job at 15SRC PresidentWeb design awardsLinux, BGP & mail
Technology and responsibility grew together.
2000—2003

The Operator

My first professional roles put the hobby under real pressure. SQL and Unix operations, logistics platforms and network administration taught me that production is different from a home lab. Someone is waiting for the system. Someone depends on the answer. I moved out at 17 and learned independence at roughly the same time I learned operational discipline.

Web developmentSQL / Unix operationsNetwork administrationIndependent at 17
2004—2013

The Engineer

I moved from keeping systems running to designing and delivering them. Networking, security, datacentres and virtualisation took me through telecommunications, banking, transport and government. I liked the difficult work: the migrations, the handovers, the environments where resilience and operability could not be an afterthought.

Dimension DataTelstraANZAustralia PostSecureWorx
2005: built the first VMware ESX environment, a defining shift toward platform architecture.
2014—2019

The Architect

By 2014, the questions had changed. It was no longer only how to build the platform, but what should be built, why it mattered and what risk the organisation was prepared to carry. I began translating between engineers, executives, risk teams and vendors, producing designs that could survive both technical scrutiny and operational reality.

Infrastructure Architect, 2014Enterprise Architect, 2016Payments Architect, 2019
2020—2026

The Advisor

The work widened again: payments, cloud operating models, security, compliance, commercial decisions and executive advice. I still care about the low-level detail, but the job is increasingly to turn complexity into a direction people can act on. The best outcome is not the cleverest diagram. It is a decision that can be understood, delivered and supported.

In my own voice

Direct, practical and still curious.

The formal titles only tell part of the story. The human thread is a preference for plain language, useful detail and honest progress.

01

Useful over impressive

I have spent years turning complicated environments into something people can understand and operate. Workshops, HLDs, LLDs, bills of material, migration runbooks and test plans are not paperwork to me. They are how a good idea becomes a safe, repeatable outcome.

02

Prepared to be candid

I am comfortable saying what I know, what I do not know and what needs to happen next. That includes acknowledging when I am running behind or when an exam result was two questions short. The important part is taking ownership, rebooking and continuing.

03

Generous with experience

I enjoy helping other people present their best work. Sometimes that means architecture advice. Sometimes it is practical detail: stage-manage the room, put the camera at eye level, proofread the CV and build a narrative you can actually speak to.

04

Technology with a human purpose

The platforms have changed from Unix and physical networks to VCF, hybrid cloud and zero-trust patterns. The purpose has not: reduce friction, protect what matters and give people a dependable foundation for their work.

What the work has taught me

Stay practical. Keep learning.

The recurring themes are less about technology brands and more about how I approach the work and the people around it.

Build before you lead.

I trust architecture more when it has been tested against implementation, operations and the people who must support it.

Simplicity scales.

Complexity arrives by itself. My contribution is to make the decision, the trade-off and the next step clear.

Technology is about people.

I have learned that a useful answer, explained plainly and offered with respect, travels further than technical correctness alone.

Progress beats perfection.

I care about quality, but experience has taught me to share work earlier, learn from feedback and avoid polishing the wrong answer.

Understand systems.
Solve problems.
Move organisations forward.

The tools changed. Curiosity, responsibility and the desire to make things work stayed the same.
